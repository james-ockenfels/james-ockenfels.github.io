I Wrote the Repair Guide for My IT Internship

This year's internship cohort is all seniors with no juniors to pass knowledge down to. So I wrote a guide to make sure that knowledge doesn't just disappear when we graduate.

I've been an IT intern at Ottumwa High School since August 2024. The internship is open to juniors and seniors, and normally it's a mix of both. The way it's supposed to work is that seniors teach the juniors throughout the year, and when the seniors graduate, the juniors move up and already know what they're doing. It's a built-in knowledge transfer system.

This year, every intern is a senior. There are seven of us, and none of us will be here next year. That means whatever we know about repairing these HP Chromebook 14 G7s walks out the door with us when we graduate unless we do something about it. So I wrote a guide.

Why It Was Needed

Before I made this, there was no written reference for any of the repairs we do. Everything was passed down verbally or figured out on the job. That works fine when you have experienced seniors showing juniors the ropes in person. It does not work when the institutional knowledge has nowhere to go.

On top of that, some of the things you learn in this role are genuinely not obvious. The two longer keyboard screws that destroy the keyboard tray if you put them in the wrong holes. The Wi-Fi chip that gets permanently fried if the battery is still connected when you work on it. The IHCC student devices that lose authentication if you Powerwash them. These are the kinds of things you only find out by making the mistake or being warned at exactly the right moment. A written guide catches all of it upfront.

What's In It

The guide covers 10 hardware issues with step-by-step repair procedures. Screen replacement, keyboard replacement, charging diagnosis and battery replacement, top and bottom shell replacements, Wi-Fi chip replacement, Powerwash, touchpad replacement, camera replacement, and display cable swap. Each section includes warnings before the steps so you see the critical stuff before you start, not partway through when it's too late.

There's also a quick reference table at the front that maps symptoms to the right section, parts diagrams pulled from the official HP service manual, a tools list, escalation guidelines, and general tips. The goal was that someone with no prior experience could pick it up on day one and actually use it.

Who's Using It

The guide has been useful in ways I didn't fully expect. Obviously it's intended for next year's interns, but my fellow seniors have been using it too, because I included tips and tricks that not everyone had picked up yet. Even my supervisor, who doesn't personally do the repairs, has been referencing it. It ended up being a useful resource right now, not just a document for future interns.

I'll be honest about something: I'm the best performing intern this year. That's not me being self-congratulatory, the IT lead told me directly and the metrics back it up. So a lot of what's in the guide reflects things I learned from doing more repairs and paying close attention to what went wrong and why. Writing it all down was a way to make sure that knowledge sticks around after I leave.

What Writing It Taught Me

Writing documentation forces you to actually understand what you are doing. There were procedures I thought I had completely down that I had to slow down and think through carefully to write out in the right order. It also made me realize how much context I had built up without noticing. Things like the magnetic bit trick for simulating lid open and close during testing, or the reason you always test before reassembling rather than after. That kind of knowledge is easy to take for granted once it is in your head.

Good documentation also has to be written for someone who doesn't already know what you know. That is genuinely harder than it sounds. The version 2.0 label on the cover exists because I revised it after getting feedback and finding things that weren't clear enough the first time.

Why I'm Proud of It

Of everything I've done in this internship, this is the thing I'm most proud of. Doing repairs is valuable, but building something that outlasts your time in a role and makes things easier for the people who come after you feels like a real contribution. The guide exists because I saw something that was needed and decided to make it. Nobody asked me to. I just did it.
