I Built a Chromebook Auto-Enroller

Faced with thousands of incoming student devices and a tight deadline, I used a Raspberry Pi Pico to automate the enterprise enrollment process.

Ahead of the upcoming school year, our district received a massive shipment of brand-new Chromebooks. It was my job to enroll as many as possible into the school domain, but processing them manually was simply too slow to meet our deadline.

The individual deployment workflow isn't complicated: unbox the device, plug it in, boot it up, walk through the setup menus, enter the enterprise enrollment credentials, type in the asset ID, apply the asset tag sticker, trigger a deep sleep command, and stick a verification label on the box. While easy on paper, repeating this sequence hundreds of times is incredibly tedious.

Why It Was Needed

The individual steps are simple enough, but scaling that process across well over 1,500 Chromebooks with only a single month to finish made it a daunting task. Working manually, an efficient intern might manage to enroll about 15 devices per hour if they are lucky. To hit our targets, I decided to build a hardware-based automation solution.

Introducing hardware automation would allow a single technician to enroll multiple devices simultaneously. By taking human typing speed and menu navigation delays out of the equation, the total number of devices processed per hour would rise drastically.

How It Works

The heart of the project is a Raspberry Pi Pico, a versatile $5 microcontroller. By flashing it with custom firmware, I turned it into a USB Human Interface Device (HID) capable of injecting keystrokes. I wrote a Ducky Script macro to simulate standard keyboard commands, handling the initial setup screens and credential entry automatically.

The script automates every menu prompt, network selection, and credential screen, driving the setup wizard all the way to the final asset ID page.

Offloading the configuration steps to the microcontroller fundamentally transformed the workflow. While a Pico automated the setup sequence on one Chromebook, I could unbox, tag, and prepare subsequent units. This parallel execution path allowed one person to operate an entire staging line.

Project Performance Metrics

As mentioned before, manual processing topped out around 15 Chromebooks an hour under ideal conditions. Incorporating the automated injection tool immediately increased my personal output to roughly 25 units per hour.

Scaling the Solution

To scale production further, I built a total of four identical modules. With two interns managing two auto-enrollers each, our collective throughput scaled up to 50 Chromebooks per hour. This optimizations significantly cut down our overall deployment schedule.

Final Deployment Results

By the end of our active deployment window, our small internship team successfully processed and validated nearly 800 ready-to-go units, far exceeding our initial manual forecasts for the month. The remaining inventory was easily handled by standard administrative staff over the summer using the infrastructure we established.
